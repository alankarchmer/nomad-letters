The Nomad Letters

For twelve years, Nick Sleep and Qais Zakaria wrote to their partners twice a year from a small office in London. Across these letters, a fund that started out buying the market’s rejects came to own a handful of companies that shared their savings with customers, and a dollar became ten. This page maps that journey.

Scorecard

Rank the companies the way Nomad did

For every passage about a company, Jev judged six things the letters care about: how cheap it looked, whether it passes its scale savings to customers, whether a founder or owner runs it, how it allocates capital, the character of its managers, and how warmly the letters write about it. Set the weights and the list re-ranks instantly, with no new model calls. The presets show how Nomad’s own priorities moved between 2002 and 2009. This describes how the letters portray each company, not whether it is a good investment.

Squares are shaded by the letters’ verdict on each trait: darker means closer to what Nomad wanted. A dashed square means the letters say nothing about that trait for the company, and it counts as neutral in the ranking. “Over time” plots how warmly each letter writes about the company. Voice

How the letters sound

Jev rated every paragraph for humour, for owning up to the authors’ own mistakes, and for criticism of the fund industry. Tone barely tracks returns: across the 24 letters, none of these measures has more than a weak link to that period’s performance. What changes is the target. Criticism of the industry runs high until 2009, then roughly halves until the final letter turns on regulation. The first letter is the plainest and the last is the funniest.

Coda

How it ended

The December 2013 letter turned out to be the last. A few months later, in 2014, Sleep and Zakaria sold the portfolio and returned the money to their partners. In a postscript written in 2021, they say they hated that final phase: selling stakes built up over years felt wrong.

They give several reasons for closing. Regulation had become heavier and blunter. They no longer wanted to explain every action, and every decision not to act, to a changing cast of overseers. And they felt they had got everything they could out of the approach. They already owned a few superb businesses and were not going to sell one to buy another cheap Philippine cement stock. They were also drawn to something new while still in their mid-forties: working out how to put their gains to use for other people.

That became the idea they call X-amount: past a certain level of wealth, more money adds little, and meaning comes from giving it away. That is why the approved letters now live on Nick Sleep’s charitable foundation website. They note, too, that these may be the only investment letters published on a charity’s site.

About this page. Built from the Full Collection of the Nomad Investment Partnership Letters to Partners, 2001–2014. All titles, summaries and notes are paraphrases written for this page, not the authors’ words. Apart from one sentence, the letters themselves are not reproduced. The authors ask readers to link to the approved version at the IGY Foundation and to respect their privacy, so this page contains no personal details beyond their names.

Performance figures are as reported in the letters: before performance fees, against the MSCI World index (net, US$). Each point uses the since-inception figure printed in that letter. Two points are derived from later tables: June 2006, when performance was sent separately, and December 2010, whose annual letter does not appear in this collection. The final letter calls itself the 25th. Word counts exclude disclaimers and housekeeping notes. Nothing here is investment advice.

How the summaries were checked. Every factual sentence in the letter summaries, stories and company notes (272 in all) was checked against the most relevant paragraphs of the letters with TypeSafe’s Jev model (jev-1.13.0). Sentences it did not clearly support were reviewed by hand against the source, and ten were rewritten to be more accurate.

How the Ideas grid was made. The default view asks Jev sixteen yes/no questions about each of the 726 paragraphs in the collection, for example whether a paragraph discusses a company passing the savings from its scale on to customers. A dot’s size is the share of the letter’s text judged to discuss the idea, weighting each paragraph by its length and by Jev’s probability. A dot appears only where at least one paragraph is judged more likely than not to discuss the idea. The other view is my original hand coding from reading the letters.

The other Jev features. Reading paths: Jev rated how central each relevant paragraph is to each idea, on a scale from a passing mention to a paragraph that sets the idea out, and the strongest are listed with their pages. Scorecard: for each of the 329 passages about a company, Jev answered six multiple-choice questions, each with an option for “the passage doesn’t say”; the ranking is computed in your browser from those answers and your weights. Influences: for each mention of a thinker, Jev chose how the reference is used and which idea it supports. Voice: Jev rated every paragraph for humour, candour and industry criticism. Ask the letters: Jev scored the twenty best keyword matches for each question. The short notes and answers were drafted from the passages Jev rated highest, and then checked against those passages with Jev.

Page links. Page numbers are positions in the 218-page approved PDF (the number printed at the foot of each page is one higher). Links open the IGY Foundation’s copy at that page in most browsers; the page numbers were taken from a copy of the same collection.
